Five Decades on the Picket Line

A History of Strikes on the Long Island Rail Road

The nation’s busiest commuter railroad has gone dark just five times in the last half‑century. Each shutdown lasted a different number of days — and was fought over a remarkably similar set of issues.

By The Upshot Published May 17, 2026

When Long Island Rail Road conductors walked off the job just after midnight on Saturday, they joined a short and unhappy lineage. In the 54 years since the Metropolitan Transportation Authority took the railroad over from the Pennsylvania Railroad’s bankrupt successor, the LIRR has been silenced by labor disputes only a handful of times. The longest of those shutdowns ran nearly two months; the shortest ended in 48 hours.

The flashpoints have barely changed. Workers want raises that keep pace with inflation and protection of their health and pension benefits. Management wants productivity concessions and lower long‑term costs. Federal law — the Railway Labor Act — keeps the two sides in a slow, scripted dance of mediation, cool‑off periods and presidential emergency boards. When that script breaks, the trains stop.

Strike by strike

What each walkout was really about

A recurring set of fights — wages against inflation, the cost of healthcare, and who pays for productivity.
1972–73 ~50 days

The last private‑era strike, in the dead of winter

Dec. 1972 → Jan. 1973 · United Transportation Union

The longest LIRR shutdown in modern memory began as the railroad was finishing its transition from the bankrupt Penn Central to public ownership under New York State. Trainmen walked out over wages and work rules in a contract fight that dragged on for roughly six weeks of winter commutes.

A shutdown so long it helped convince Albany that the LIRR could not be run as a private business.

1980 2 days

The first walkout of the MTA era

June 1980 · Coalition of rail unions

Eight years after the MTA absorbed the railroad, contract talks collapsed and crews briefly went out. The strike was settled in 48 hours after federal mediators stepped in, setting a pattern: the political cost of a stopped Long Island commute is so high that Albany and Washington move quickly.

A 48‑hour proof of concept: stop the trains, and the bargaining table fills up.

1987 11 days

Eleven unions, eleven days, and a call from the White House

Jan. 18 – 29, 1987 · 11 unions

The longest stoppage of the MTA era began on a Sunday in January, when 11 unions walked out demanding higher wages and better health and pension benefits. Commuters scrambled for buses and carpools for a week and a half before President Ronald Reagan ordered a 60‑day cooling‑off period under the Railway Labor Act, forcing crews back to work.

Ended not at the bargaining table, but by presidential order.

1994 2 days

Two and a half years without a contract, two days without trains

June 17 – 19, 1994 · Conductors & maintenance crews

Conductors and maintenance workers, who had gone two and a half years without a new contract, struck on a Friday. Governor Mario M. Cuomo and his aides intervened over the weekend and imposed a settlement on LIRR management, ending the walkout before the Monday morning rush.

It would be the last strike for nearly a generation — 32 years of labor peace.

2026 Ongoing

A one‑point gap on wages stops 300,000 commutes

Began 12:01 a.m. Saturday, May 16, 2026 · 5 unions · ~3,500 workers

Engineers, electricians, signal inspectors, machinists and ticket agents — more than half the railroad’s workforce — walked off after months of bargaining failed to close a roughly one‑percentage‑point gap on wages. The unions are seeking a raise of about 5 percent to keep pace with inflation; the MTA has offered close to 4.5 percent, but tied to a new requirement that future hires pay more for their health insurance than current workers do.

The dispute is almost a carbon copy of 1987: wages against inflation, plus the cost of healthcare.

The Upshot · Analysis

What the pattern shows

5
Strikes since 1972

In 54 years of public ownership, the LIRR has been silenced by labor disputes five times — roughly once a decade until a long quiet stretch.

67
Total days halted

Add up every shutdown since 1972 and the railroad has been dark for the equivalent of about nine work‑weeks total.

3
Ended by intervention

Of the five strikes, three were settled by political muscle — a governor, a president, or a federal cooling‑off order — not by the parties themselves.

Read end to end, the LIRR’s strike history tells a tight, repeating story. Almost every walkout has been triggered by some combination of three things: wages falling behind inflation, rising healthcare and pension costs, and management proposals that ask new hires to accept worse terms than current workers. The 1987 dispute, the 1994 dispute and the 2026 dispute could be summarized with nearly the same sentence.

The second pattern is about who actually ends the strikes. The Railway Labor Act gives elected officials extraordinary leverage: a president can impose a 60‑day cooling‑off period, as Ronald Reagan did in 1987, and a governor can lean on the MTA, as Mario Cuomo did in 1994. That leverage cuts both ways. It keeps shutdowns short — four of five lasted 11 days or fewer — but it also gives both sides an incentive to hold out for political intervention rather than settle at the table.

The third pattern is the silence between the bars on the chart. After 1994, the LIRR went 32 years without a strike, the longest stretch of labor peace in its public history. That run ended this weekend over what is, on paper, one of the smallest gaps the two sides have ever fought about: roughly a single percentage point on wages, plus the price new employees will pay for their health benefits. The dollar amounts are modest. The disruption — for 300,000 daily commuters and the regional economy that depends on them — is not.
